Every static-site-generator comparison tells you Hugo is fastest. Almost none of them tell you
*in what shape* — and that's the part that decides whether your build still works two years from
now, when the site has 3,000 posts instead of 80.

So I built the same site three times, with byte-identical markdown, and measured it properly:
four corpus sizes, cold builds, medians over repeated runs, then a least-squares fit separating
**fixed startup cost** from **marginal cost per page**.

The headline ranking is the boring, expected one. The interesting result is the decomposition,
and one number in it surprised me.

## Methodology

Stated up front so you can attack it.

**Hardware:** 6-core `aarch64` VM under QEMU (CPU implementer `0x61` — Apple Silicon host),
3.3 GB RAM, Ubuntu 26.04.1 LTS, kernel 7.0.0. This is deliberately *not* a fast machine. ARM
build numbers are scarce and CI runners are frequently this size or smaller.

**Versions:** Hugo `v0.166.0+extended`, Eleventy `3.1.6`, Astro `5.18.2`, Node `v26.9.0` — all
installed [without root](/devtool-digest/posts/dev-toolchain-without-root/), which is its own story on ARM.

**Corpus:** generated by a script with a fixed random seed, so the same 2,500 posts exist on any
machine that runs it. Each post has frontmatter (title, date, two tags, summary), an H1, two H2
sections, ~140 words of prose, a fenced JS code block and a markdown table — about 1,954 bytes
each. Real enough to exercise the markdown parser, small enough to isolate generator overhead.

**Templates:** minimal and equivalent in all three — the same `<title>`, `<h1>`, and rendered
body. Verified by diffing actual output:

```html
<!-- Hugo -->    <!DOCTYPE html><html><head><title>Benchmark Post 1</title></head><body><h1>Benchmark Post 1</h1><h1 id="post-1">Post 1</h1>
<!-- Eleventy --><!DOCTYPE html><html><head><title>Benchmark Post 1</title></head><body><h1>Benchmark Post 1</h1><h1>Post 1</h1>
<!-- Astro -->   <!DOCTYPE html><html><head><title>Benchmark Post 1</title></head> <body><h1>Benchmark Post 1</h1><h1 id="post-1">Post 1</h1>
```

**Timing:** every run is **cold** — output directory and generator cache wiped first. One
sacrificial run before each measured set warms the OS page cache so the first timed run isn't
punished for being first. Wall clock around process exit, which includes Node startup, because
that's what you actually wait for.

**Not measured:** dev-server HMR, incremental rebuilds, image pipelines, partial hydration.
Those matter enormously and aren't comparable across these minimal configs. This measures cold
full-site build throughput, nothing more.

## The raw numbers

Median cold build, 3 runs per cell:

| pages | Hugo | Eleventy | Astro |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 0.055s | 0.377s | 1.180s |
| 300 | 0.102s | 0.371s | 1.865s |
| 1,000 | 0.244s | 0.638s | 4.420s |
| 2,500 | 0.503s | 1.322s | 11.762s |

Relative to Hugo:

| pages | Hugo | Eleventy | Astro |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 1.0x | 6.9x | 21.6x |
| 300 | 1.0x | 3.6x | 18.3x |
| 1,000 | 1.0x | 2.6x | 18.1x |
| 2,500 | 1.0x | 2.6x | 23.4x |

Note that the Eleventy multiplier **improves** as the site grows — 6.9x down to 2.6x — while
Astro's stays roughly flat. That's the first hint that these numbers are measuring two different
things mixed together.

At 2,500 pages Hugo also emitted **2,514** HTML files against the others' 2,500: it generates
tag and category list pages the minimal Eleventy and Astro configs don't. It does 14 pages of
extra work and still wins by 2.6x. That asymmetry favours the others, and Hugo wins anyway.

## The decomposition, which is the actual story

Fit each generator to `time = startup + per_page × N`:

| Generator | Fixed startup | Marginal per page | R² | 10,000 pages (extrapolated) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Hugo | 45 ms | **0.185 ms** | 0.9976 | ~1.9 s |
| Eleventy | 278 ms | **0.409 ms** | 0.9881 | ~4.4 s |
| Astro | 476 ms | **4.441 ms** | 0.9950 | ~44.9 s |

R² above 0.988 for all three means build time is **linear** in page count across this range for
every generator. Nobody is quadratic. Good.

{{< chart-ssg-fixed-marginal >}}

Now the two costs separate cleanly:

**Eleventy's real per-page cost is only 2.2x Hugo's.** Not 6.9x. At small corpus sizes Eleventy
looks far slower than it is, because you're mostly timing Node and Eleventy booting up. At 100
pages, **87% of an Eleventy build is fixed startup** — you are benchmarking `require()`, not the
markdown pipeline.

**Astro's per-page cost is 24x Hugo's**, and that is a genuine per-page difference that does not
amortise. It's the one number here that compounds: at 10,000 pages the fit projects ~45 seconds
per cold build.

I verified the fitted intercepts independently rather than trusting the regression, by timing
each tool's own no-op:

```
node -e ''                      11 ms
hugo version                    18 ms
npx astro --version            127 ms
npx @11ty/eleventy --version   201 ms
```

Same ordering, same magnitude as the fitted startup costs. The model isn't inventing anything.

## Where the time actually goes, by site size

| pages | Hugo | Eleventy | Astro |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 71% startup | **87% startup** | 52% startup |
| 300 | 45% startup | 69% startup | 26% startup |
| 1,000 | 20% startup | 40% startup | 10% startup |
| 2,500 | 9% startup | 21% startup | 4% startup |

This table is the practical one, and it's why the "which SSG is fastest" question is usually
asked wrong:

- **Under ~300 pages** — the overwhelming majority of blogs — you are mostly measuring process
  startup, and every option here builds in under two seconds. The speed difference is real but
  irrelevant to your life. Pick on templating language, ecosystem, and whether you want
  components.
- **Past ~1,000 pages** startup stops mattering and marginal cost dominates. Now the 24x is what
  you feel, on every CI push, forever.

## A caveat I have to flag on the Astro numbers

Astro was measurably noisier than the others. Spread across the three runs at 2,500 pages:

```
Hugo       [0.478, 0.503, 0.551]   spread 0.073s
Eleventy   [1.322, 1.361, 1.288]   spread 0.074s
Astro      [12.598, 11.762, 11.596] spread 1.002s
```

Astro's spread is **13x** the other two in absolute terms. On a memory-constrained VM (3.3 GB,
~1 GB free) a Vite-based build has far more opportunity to contend for memory and CPU than a
single Go binary does. I report the median for exactly this reason, but treat Astro's numbers as
having wider error bars than the others.

This is also the honest framing of the comparison overall: **Astro is not trying to win this
benchmark.** It's doing a fundamentally different job — component islands, framework
integration, partial hydration — and it pays for that at build time. Measuring it purely on
markdown throughput is fair only if markdown throughput is what you need.

## Pitfalls I hit, so you don't

**Eleventy resolves `dir.includes` relative to the *input* directory, not the project root.**
With `input: "content"`, a top-level `_includes/` is invisible — Eleventy looks for
`content/_includes/`. The error message is at least explicit about it:

```
[11ty] You're trying to use a layout that does not exist: content/_includes/base.njk
```

The fix is a relative escape, and the [official docs flag it with a warning
sign](https://www.11ty.dev/docs/config/) (*"⚠️ This value is relative to your input directory"*):

```js
dir: { input: "content", output: "_site", includes: "../_includes" }
```

**Hugo silently skips files that start with an underscore.** My first test page was
`_afftest.md` and it simply never appeared in the output — no error, no warning, just absent.

**Hugo also silently skips future-dated content.** With `buildFuture = false` (the default), a
post dated 09:00 when the clock says 07:25 doesn't build. Combined with the underscore rule,
that's two separate silent no-ops before you get a single line of diagnostic output.

**Astro markdown pages need `layout:` in frontmatter**, per file — there's no directory-level
default like Eleventy's data files or Hugo's `_default`. Fine when a script generates your
corpus; tedious by hand.

## What I'd actually tell you to do

**Fewer than ~500 pages:** build time is not a real input to this decision. All three finish
before you've looked away. Choose on what you'll be writing every day — Go templates, Nunjucks,
or components.

**1,000+ pages and growing:** the marginal numbers are your forecast. Hugo at 0.185 ms/page
stays under two seconds at 10,000 posts. Eleventy at 0.409 ms/page stays comfortable too, and
buys you a JavaScript ecosystem — that 2.2x is a much better trade than the 6.9x headline at
small sizes suggests.

**Choosing Astro:** do it for islands and component authoring, which are real advantages this
benchmark can't see. Just go in knowing the build-time bill, and check it against your own
content volume rather than a blog post's 300-page demo — including mine.

**The meta-point:** a single-corpus-size benchmark can't distinguish a startup cost from a
per-page cost, and those two have completely opposite implications as you grow. If a comparison
gives you one number at one size, it hasn't told you what you need to know.

## Reproduce it

Everything is committed —
[`gen_corpus.py`, `benchmark.py`, and `scaling.py`](https://github.com/horushermezzz-byte/devtool-digest/tree/main/bench).
Fixed seed, so the corpus is identical on your machine:

```sh
python3 gen_corpus.py 2500 ./content   # deterministic corpus
RUNS=3 python3 scaling.py 100,300,1000,2500
```

If your numbers differ meaningfully — especially on x86, where I'd expect the gaps to narrow —
[open an issue](https://github.com/horushermezzz-byte/devtool-digest/issues) with your
`scaling.json`. I'd like to collect results across architectures, because ARM data for this is
genuinely thin.
